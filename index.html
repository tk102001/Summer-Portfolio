A Summer of Learning How to Read a City

A personal reflection on twelve weeks at Columbia GSAPP.

01

I Arrived.

The city didn't ask what I already knew. It just kept moving, and I had to decide how fast to catch up.

I arrived in New York thinking urban design was about designing cities. Throughout the semester, my team and I returned to the same part of Brooklyn for every major assignment. We walked from DUMBO along the waterfront, through Cobble Hill and Boerum Hill, and up Flatbush Avenue. Revisiting the same streets again and again taught me that cities reveal themselves through repeated observation rather than a single visit.

02

I Observed.

Observation became the first real tool I was given. Not sketching — looking, slowly, until the site told me something I hadn't asked it.

The waterfront map took three weeks to feel honest. Every draft before that was still describing the site the way I expected it to look.

03

Reading Brooklyn

By returning to the same neighbourhood through different assignments, I stopped looking for answers and started looking for relationships. Every drawing revealed another layer of how Brooklyn worked.

04

I Learned New Ways of Seeing.

Some places revealed themselves only after slowing down. Motion captured relationships that drawings alone could not.

05

I Began Designing.

Brooklyn Forest Friends

Brooklyn Forest Friends was our Studio Assignment 2 publication. Rather than presenting the project as a single board, we designed it as a 19-page illustrated booklet that introduces children to Brooklyn's urban ecology through storytelling, mapping and play. Click below to read the complete book.

Understanding Heat Problems in Brooklyn

Three short videos exploring heat, comfort and environmental conditions across our Brooklyn study area.

Every assignment asked me to represent the city differently. Together they changed how I learned to see.
06

I Left Changed.

The semester didn't leave me with certainty. It left me with better questions, and a different way of looking at cities.

The semester ended.

The questions didn't.

How do we represent a city without simplifying it?

How can design make complexity understandable?

Can drawings become conversations rather than conclusions?

How do we communicate urban systems to people who don't speak the language of planning?

If every neighbourhood tells a different story, what does it mean to design for an entire city?

I arrived hoping to learn how to design cities.

I leave wanting to help people read them.

Tanvi Karwa
